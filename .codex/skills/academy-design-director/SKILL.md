# Academy Design Director · La PRE Digital

Create a product that makes a prospective student want to begin and an active student want to return. Design must support trust, focus, progress, and conversion—not decoration alone.

## Product context

La PRE Digital is a pre-university preparation platform for the UNT. Its product promise is a guided, personalized route made of diagnostics, materials, activities, exercises, simulations, progress analysis, and—in higher plans—guardian reporting.

Preserve real product behavior and plan entitlements. Do not create fictitious statistics, benefits, outcomes, payment assurances, or educational claims. Use existing data and labels when a screen is connected to backend state.

## Start with the job of the screen

Inspect the related component, route, available data, and current styles before editing. Determine the screen's primary job:

- Landing: communicate the outcome, demonstrate the product, build trust, and direct the visitor to one primary next step.
- Plans and checkout: make differences, price, value, and selection confidence immediately clear.
- Dashboard: eliminate uncertainty by prioritizing the learner's next best action and visible progress.
- Learning: create concentration, feedback, momentum, and a satisfying completion state.
- Profile and administration: privilege clarity, useful density, and safe understandable actions.

Never fix an empty screen only by increasing font sizes, whitespace, gradients, or generic cards. Add information that answers: *what do I get, what should I do next, how am I progressing, and why is this worth it?*

## Visual direction

- Use a deliberate hierarchy: one dominant message/action per viewport, supporting proof second, secondary paths third.
- Pair an editorial display treatment for emotional headlines with a highly legible UI sans-serif for navigation, data, forms, and learning instructions.
- Keep La PRE Digital's deep institutional blue, energetic orange, clean surfaces, and blue progress accent consistent. Reserve orange for decisive actions, progress moments, priority, and selected states.
- Make cards earn their space: they must contain a decision, meaningful status, content preview, data, or action. Avoid repeated hollow boxes.
- Create depth with grid composition, contrast, subtle borders, carefully controlled shadows, progress visuals, and UI-native motifs. Avoid stock-like ornamentation or decorative clutter.
- On desktop, use the horizontal space with an intentional grid. On mobile, preserve hierarchy and reachable actions rather than shrinking a desktop layout into a thin column.

## Conversion and content architecture

For public pages, include only the applicable parts of this sequence:

1. A specific preparation promise with a clear primary CTA.
2. Product proof: diagnostic, adaptive route, session preview, exercise/simulation, or progress feedback.
3. A concrete explanation of the student journey.
4. Plan comparison that makes upgrading understandable.
5. A final CTA with truthful objection-reducing context already supported by the product.

Use concrete learning language. Explain what a learner can do—diagnose, follow a route, solve a timed exercise, complete a simulation, see feedback—instead of vague promises about learning.

## Learning quality bar

- The next recommended action must expose topic, estimated time, level, and payoff.
- Show completed and remaining work meaningfully, with a clear change after an activity finishes.
- Every activity requires start, in-progress, feedback, completion, and return-to-route states.
- Differentiate sessions, exercises, simulations, and analysis visually and functionally; do not reuse a card with only a label change.
- Keep the language aspirational but mature for senior-secondary and pre-university students.

## Interaction and implementation

- Every visible interactive control must work, route coherently, or be disabled with an explicit explanation.
- Include hover, focus, selected, loading, empty, error, and completion states where they apply.
- Maintain semantic controls, keyboard focus, labels, readable contrast, and responsive layout.
- Build the project after changes. When visual changes are substantial, inspect the affected route at a desktop width and a narrow width before delivering.
- Do not alter authentication, subscription, payment, roles, or access rules unless the user explicitly asks for a behavioral change.
